# Bearing: A Philosopher's Lens

Build a polished, responsive web application called Bearing.

Bearing helps people examine real decisions, habits, relationships, fears, and life problems through the ideas of major philosophers. A visitor answers several short questions, receives one free reading through Aristotle’s philosophy, and can eventually become a paid member to consult the full collection of philosophers.

The product should feel like a serious philosophical publication or private Roman study. It must not resemble an AI chatbot, a self help app, a children’s history site, or a generic SaaS landing page.

1. Brand positioning

Name: Bearing

Primary headline: Find your bearing.

Supporting phrase: Clear thinking for real life.

The name refers to finding direction, understanding where you stand, and choosing how to move forward.

Bearing should present philosophy as a practical way to think more clearly, not as trivia, academic study, motivational content, or mystical guidance.

The application should say that it interprets a situation through a philosopher’s ideas. It should not falsely claim that the historical philosopher is literally speaking.

Do not use em dashes anywhere in the interface copy.

Avoid common AI language such as:

Unlock your potential

Embark on a journey

Timeless wisdom meets modern technology

Transform your life

Discover the best version of yourself

AI powered insights

Personalized wisdom at your fingertips

The writing should be direct, intelligent, calm, and human.

Preferred font stack:

Headlines: Palatino, Book Antiqua, Georgia, or a high quality Roman serif

Large headlines should have tight spacing and dramatic scale. Small labels should use uppercase letters with generous tracking.

Do not introduce a modern geometric sans serif. The entire identity depends on the classical typography.

12. Philosopher content

Aristotle

Dates: 384–322 BC
School: Virtue Ethics
Core principle: A good life is built one habit at a time.
Card summary: Use your daily choices to become the person you want to be.

Overview:

“Aristotle believed the point of life was to flourish: to use your abilities well and become a person of good character. You do that through habit. Each choice is practice for the next one. His approach is useful when you know what matters to you, but your routines or priorities keep pulling you away from it.”

Four principles:

Build character by doing
“You become brave by doing brave things, generous by giving, and dependable by keeping your word.”

Aim for the right measure
“Too much and too little can both miss the mark. The right response depends on the person and the moment.”

Use practical judgment
“Life rarely gives you a perfect rule. Good judgment means seeing what this situation actually calls for.”

Judge the whole life
“A good life is more than pleasure or success. It includes work, friendship, purpose, and how you treat people.”

Best for thinking about:

“Habits, purpose, leadership, relationships, and finding balance.”

Question to sit with:

“What habit is this choice building?”

Socrates

Dates: 470–399 BC
School: Socratic Inquiry
Core principle: Question the story you keep telling yourself.
Card summary: Test your assumptions before they make your decisions for you.

Overview:

“Socrates rarely gave people an answer. He asked them to explain what they meant, then followed the logic until weak assumptions showed themselves. The point was not to win an argument. It was to think honestly enough to live honestly. His method is useful when you feel stuck between options or keep returning to the same conflict.”

Four principles:

Examine your life
“Do not let habit, fear, or other people’s expectations choose your life without being questioned.”

Admit what you do not know
“Being unsure is not a weakness. It is the point where honest thinking can begin.”

Define your words
“Before arguing about success, loyalty, justice, or love, ask what you actually mean by them.”

Follow the contradiction
“When two beliefs do not fit together, do not ignore the gap. It usually contains the real problem.”

Best for thinking about:

“Confusion, recurring arguments, major decisions, and beliefs that no longer fit your life.”

Question to sit with:

“What are you assuming without checking?”

Marcus Aurelius

Dates: 121–180 AD
School: Stoicism
Core principle: You cannot control what happens. You can control what you do next.
Card summary: Stay steady under pressure and act on what is actually yours.

Overview:

“Marcus Aurelius was an emperor trying to remain decent under pressure. His private notes became a guide to staying calm, doing your duty, and not letting praise, criticism, anger, or fear run your life. He reminds you that events are not yours to command. Your attention, judgment, and next action are.”

Four principles:

Pause before you judge
“What happened and what you tell yourself about it are not the same thing. Leave room between them.”

Do the useful thing
“Status and resentment waste attention. Ask what would help the people and the work in front of you.”

Return to today
“The past is over and the future is not here. Your only place to act is the present.”

Keep your ego in proportion
“A wider view makes most insults, delays, and anxieties smaller. It also makes the important work clearer.”

Best for thinking about:

“Pressure, responsibility, criticism, grief, anger, and uncertain outcomes.”

Question to sit with:

“What is yours to do today?”

Epictetus

Dates: approximately 50–135 AD
School: Stoicism
Core principle: Put your effort where you have a say.
Card summary: Stop spending energy on outcomes and people you cannot control.

Overview:

“Epictetus knew how little control a person can have over circumstances. Born into slavery, he built his philosophy around a hard distinction: some things are up to us and some are not. Other people, reputation, and final outcomes are never fully ours. How we respond is. His approach turns wasted worry into deliberate action.”

Four principles:

Separate what you control
“Your choices and judgments are yours. Other people, outcomes, and reputation are not.”

Do not trust the first thought
“A frightening first impression is not a final verdict. Stop and test it before you react.”

Keep your choices your own
“Someone else can limit your options. They do not get to decide what kind of person you will be.”

Use difficulty as practice
“Every obstacle gives you a chance to practice patience, courage, restraint, or clear thinking.”

Best for thinking about:

“Anxiety, rejection, difficult people, discipline, and outcomes you cannot control.”

Question to sit with:

“Which part of this is actually up to you?”

Plato

Dates: 428–348 BC
School: Platonism
Core principle: What seems true is not always the whole truth.
Card summary: Step back from appearances and ask which ideal should guide you.

Overview:

“Plato believed that everyday appearances can hide deeper truths. In his famous cave, people mistake shadows for reality because shadows are all they have known. His philosophy asks you to step back, question what looks obvious, and decide which ideals deserve to guide your life: truth, justice, beauty, or goodness.”

Four principles:

Look past appearances
“What is familiar, popular, or visible may still be incomplete. Ask what you are not seeing.”

Name the ideal
“Ideas such as justice and beauty give you a standard for judging an imperfect situation.”

Put the self in order
“A good life lets reason guide ambition and appetite instead of letting the loudest impulse win.”

Keep moving toward the good
“Learning and love can move you beyond possession and status toward something less selfish.”

Best for thinking about:

“Values, identity, justice, love, long term direction, and hard tradeoffs.”

Question to sit with:

“What would the best version of this look like?”

Seneca

Dates: 4 BC–65 AD
School: Roman Stoicism
Core principle: Life is long enough if you stop wasting it.
Card summary: Use your time well, prepare for setbacks, and hold success lightly.

Overview:

“Seneca wrote for people with careers, money, ambition, friendships, and grief. He kept returning to time: the one thing we give away while carefully guarding everything else. His version of Stoicism helps you prepare for setbacks, question your anger, and enjoy success without becoming dependent on it.”

Four principles:

Guard your time
“A full calendar is not the same as a full life. Spend your hours on purpose.”

Prepare for setbacks
“Thinking about loss before it arrives can make you less afraid and more ready.”

Hold success lightly
“Comfort and money can be useful. They become dangerous when you cannot imagine being okay without them.”

Question your anger
“Anger grows from the story you tell about an event. Change the story and the emotion can change with it.”

Best for thinking about:

“Time, ambition, burnout, money, anger, mortality, and fear of losing what you have built.”

Question to sit with:

“If your time were limited, would this still deserve it?”

18. Actual product functionality

Build the project so the static prototype can become a working paid product.

Use Supabase or an equivalent service for:

User accounts

Secure authentication

Saved inquiries

Saved philosophical readings

Subscription status

Reading history

User privacy controls

Use Stripe for membership payments.

The free tier should include one complete Aristotle reading. Paid membership should unlock all philosophers and allow users to compare multiple philosophical perspectives on the same question.

Keep AI keys, payment keys, and system prompts on the server. Never expose them in the client.

Each philosopher should have a carefully written system profile grounded in their actual philosophy. The AI should apply the philosopher’s method to the user’s situation without producing fake quotations.

Each generated reading should contain:

What the philosopher would notice

What assumption they would question

Which philosophical principle applies

One practical exercise

One question to continue considering

A short note explaining that the reading is for reflection, not clinical, legal, medical, or financial advice

Do not create an open ended chatbot as the main experience. The product’s advantage is structured guidance with minimal prompting.

21. Interaction and accessibility requirements

Use semantic HTML

Include visible focus states

Give portraits useful alt text

Support keyboard navigation

Allow Escape to close modals

Trap focus inside open modals

Lock background scrolling while a modal is open

Respect reduced motion preferences

Maintain strong text contrast

Use subtle transitions only

Use smooth scrolling for page navigation

Do not sacrifice readability for the papyrus effect

The final result should look like an established philosophical publication that happens to be interactive. It should feel intelligent, restrained, distinctive, and credible.
